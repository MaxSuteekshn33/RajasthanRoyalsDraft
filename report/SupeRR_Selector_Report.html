Round 1 — Methodology & Selection Report  ·  IPL 2022–2026 and T20 Internationals
Executive summary

Pressure is measurable. “Clutch” mostly is not.

We scored every delivery in the data pack on a continuous, role-relative Pressure Index, then measured each player against what the situation normally yields rather than against a raw average. Three findings shaped the model — and the third changed it fundamentally.

01

Pressure doesn’t slow batters down — it gets them out

Run rate moves -4.5% across pressure bands, but the dismissal rate doubles (+101%) and runs per dismissal fall 52%. Pressure charges a risk premium, not a scoring penalty. A metric built on strike rate cannot see it.

02

Pressure is role-relative, not a phase of the innings

A run of dots strangles the batter but is pressure the bowler is applying. We score every ball twice, once from each side. Making the build-up pillar asymmetric is what finally made the bowling signal behave.

03

Buy quality, not a reputation for bottle

Overall impact repeats across halves of the data (r = 0.48). The clutch premium does not (r = 0.01). So we shrink each player’s pressure record toward their own standard, not the league’s.

What this ranking is — and is not

It identifies the players with the highest expected output when the Pressure Index is high. It is not a temperament ranking. Because the clutch premium barely repeats, a player with a glamorous highlight reel and ordinary underlying numbers is a trap — and the model is built to say so.

Section 1

Defining pressure

The brief left the definition to us. We made two structural choices up front.

Pressure is continuous, not a death-overs switch. Treating overs 16–20 as “pressure” and everything else as “not” discards the hardest ball of a collapsing powerplay and includes a dead rubber’s final over. Every delivery is scored 0–100.

Pressure is role-relative. The same delivery is agony for one player and opportunity for the other, so we compute PI_bat and PI_bowl separately.

PI = 0.40·Leverage + 0.25·Adversity + 0.20·Scarcity + 0.15·Build-up
     ×  knockout multiplier (1.08)
PillarWeight What it captures and how it is computed
Leverage40% How much this ball can swing the result. Expected |ΔWin Probability| from one delivery, obtained by enumerating the eight possible outcomes (0/1/2/3/4/6, wicket, extra) against their state-conditional probabilities.
Adversity25% How badly your own side is placed: 1 − WP(own team). This is the pillar that makes pressure role-relative.
Scarcity20% How much batting resource is spent, read off a fitted Duckworth–Lewis-style resource curve, so balls and wickets are priced on one scale.
Build-up15% Asymmetric. Dots and wickets for the batter; runs and boundaries conceded for the bowler. A one-ball lag means a delivery is never scored using its own outcome.

Leverage carries the most weight because it is the one pillar derived from a fitted statistical model; the other three capture cricketing texture a win-probability model alone misses. A ball scoring PI ≥ 65 is classified as a pressure ball (12.9% of deliveries for batting, 12.1% for bowling) — but that threshold is used for reporting only. The ratings themselves use continuous weighting.

Section 2

Measuring performance

Strike rate and economy are the wrong currency: what a good ball is worth depends entirely on the situation. We price everything in runs above expectation.

batter impact = (runs − xRuns) − wicketCost × (out − xWicket)
bowler impact = (xRuns − runs) + wicketCost × (wicket − xWicket)
  • xRuns / xWicket — what the average player manages in that exact situation, from hierarchical empirical means over (context, phase, wickets in hand, pressure band, pace/spin), falling back to coarser keys below 200 deliveries. Critically, the pressure band is part of the conditioning: pressure balls are judged against pressure norms, which stops the death overs flattering everyone who bats there.
  • wicketCost — the batting resource a dismissal destroys, read off the resource curve: ~16 runs in the powerplay down to 2–4 at the death. Fully data-derived.
  • Run-outs are excluded from bowler credit, byes and leg-byes from bowler debit, and non-striker dismissals from the striker’s record.

Rating construction — three deliberate choices

1 · Weight, don’t filter

A hard PI ≥ 65 cut-off left only 66 batters with 100+ pressure balls — too thin to rank on. Instead every delivery contributes with weight u^1.5 where u = (PI − 45)/55, so the estimate uses all 201k balls while still being dominated by the hard ones. Recency is applied on top (0.78^seasons-ago), because this is an auction decision.

2 · Shrink toward the player, not the league

Because overall form repeats (r = 0.48) and clutch does not (r = 0.01), the statistically correct prior for a player’s pressure performance is their own overall record:

overall_hat = shrink(overall impact, league mean, k = 782)
press_hat   = shrink(pressure impact, overall_hat, k = 245)

Ballasts k are derived from measured split-half reliability (k = n̄(1−r)/r), not chosen by taste. This is also what a good selector does by instinct: assume a quality player will cope until their pressure record says otherwise, loudly and repeatedly.

3 · Let components earn their weight

Each component’s base weight is scaled by the square root of its measured reliability, then renormalised.

ComponentBatting weight Bowling weight

The weights differ by role because the reliability differs by role — bowling consistency repeats (r = 0.24), batting consistency does not (r = 0.01).

Section 3

Does the index actually measure pressure?

A pressure definition is only meaningful if performance demonstrably degrades as it rises. Four tests: does output fall, does it survive controlling for phase, does it depend on any single pillar, and does the shortlist hold up?

Test 2 — The signal survives controlling for phase

The obvious objection is that this merely measures “which over is it”. So we split deliveries into top and bottom pressure terciles within each phase. The gap survives everywhere — and for bowlers it only behaved once the build-up pillar was made asymmetric.

Test 3 — No single pillar carries the result

Build-up is the pillar most at risk of flattering the result, because recent scoring autocorrelates with the current ball — a slow pitch produces both. Removing it shrinks the gap by roughly a third but does not remove it. Leverage alone separates almost nothing outside the death overs, which is precisely why the index is a composite and not just a win-probability model.

Test 4 — Stability of the shortlist

Thirteen variants: pressure floor 35/45/55, linear/convex/steep weighting, recency 0.60/0.78/1.00, three component-weighting schemes, three alternative pillar balances, and a stricter eligibility bar.

Reliability and limitations

Matches are dealt alternately by date into two halves. A metric that measures skill correlates across them; one that measures variance does not. Spearman–Brown converts the half-sample correlation to full-length reliability.

Known limitations

  1. This is not a temperament ranking. It says these twenty have the highest expected output when PI is high. Reading it as “strongest nerve” over-reads the evidence.
  2. Pressure samples are small even across five seasons. Error bars on any individual estimate are wide — hence the consensus approach and the published rank swings.
  3. No scouting data: injuries, fitness, contract status, auction availability or age. Nor pitch and dew, which matter most in exactly the death-over situations the model weights heaviest.
  4. First-innings pressure is weaker. The chase model is strong (Brier 0.128); the first-innings model much weaker (0.221), because without a target the state is less informative. Batters who do their best work setting totals are likely under-credited.
  5. Build-up partly measures conditions, not the player — quantified in Test 3 rather than buried.
  6. Residual context bias for players whose record sits almost entirely in one competition — flagged per player in the dossiers.

Data integrity

Reconstruction check

806 of 838 matches reproduce the official result margin exactly from our rebuilt innings totals. The remainder are D/L-adjusted or abandoned, plus one wicket-count discrepancy.

A real bug, caught and fixed

Extras tagged other were being counted as legal deliveries, pushing 150 innings past 120 balls and corrupting every downstream state. We re-anchored the ball count to the official over number, eliminating all impossible states.

The source file is truncated

It stops at exactly 201,000 rows, mid-innings. 16 matches in matches.csv have no deliveries and the final match is incomplete; all are excluded from model fitting.
